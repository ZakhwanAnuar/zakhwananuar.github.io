CHAPTER 1 — ETHICS AND THE UNDERLYING PHILOSOPHIES 🔴⭐ HIGH YIELD

1.1 Core definitions (know these cold — frequent MCQs + short answers)

Term Definition
Society An association of people organized under a system of rules, designed to advance the good of its members over time.
Morality A society's rules of conduct — principles describing what people ought / ought not to do in various situations (right/wrong, good/bad, fair/unfair).
Ethics The rational examination of morality; a branch of philosophy that evaluates whether people's behavior is morally right or wrong, using reason and logic (not gut feeling).
Etiquette Conventional social rules of polite behavior (manners) — not about right vs wrong morally. E.g., saying "please," CC-ing your boss on emails.
Moral values Fundamental beliefs about right and wrong (honesty, justice, respect, integrity, compassion).
Norms/practices Informal rules/expected behaviors (punctuality, greetings, queuing).
Ethical point of view Recognizing that other people and their core values are also worthy of respect.

Key distinctions to memorize: - Society = the group of people + rules. Morality = the rules of conduct. Ethics = the study/examination of that morality. - Moral but not ethical: a person can act on good intent yet break formal ethical rules (e.g., a doctor treating a non-paying patient in an emergency — morally good, may violate policy). - Illegal vs unethical (common MCQ/short question): - Illegal but not unethical: downloading pirated software to study because you genuinely can't afford it; jaywalking to help someone. - Unethical but not illegal: lying to a friend; a company using misleading (but technically legal) ads; cheating on a partner.

1.2 The 6 ethical theories

Slides group them as workable vs non-workable — examiners love asking "explain why X is a non-workable theory."

# Theory Core idea Workable?
1a Subjective Relativism Each individual decides what's right/wrong for themselves ❌ Non-workable
1b Cultural Relativism Right/wrong is defined by a society's/culture's customs ❌ Non-workable
2 Ethical Egoism An action is right if it serves the individual actor's self-interest ❌ Non-workable
3 Divine Command Theory Right/wrong is whatever God/religion commands ❌ Non-workable
4 Kantianism An action is right if done from duty and its rule can be universalized; people must be treated as ends ✅ Workable
5 Utilitarianism An action is right if it produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number ✅ Workable
6 Social Contract Theory Morality = the set of rules rational people agree to live by for mutual benefit ✅ Workable

1️⃣ Relativism

Subjective Relativism — "what is morally right is determined by the individual." No one can tell anyone else they're wrong. - Why non-workable: leads to contradictions (two people can both be "right" while contradicting each other); allows any behavior to be justified; can't resolve disputes; confuses tolerance with morality (tolerance ≠ allowing harm).

Cultural Relativism — "what is morally right is determined by a society's/culture's customs." Morality varies by culture; outsiders can't judge. - Why non-workable: no universal moral standard → we couldn't condemn clearly evil practices (slavery, genocide) in other cultures; doesn't explain how guidelines evolve; the fact that cultures disagree doesn't prove there's no right answer; societies are not morally infallible.

2️⃣ Ethical Egoism

Each person should act in their own long-term self-interest. - Argument for: the community can benefit when individuals pursue their own well-being; people know their own interests best. - Why non-workable: can justify harming others for personal gain (e.g., spreading rumors about a rival to get promoted); it's not a genuine moral theory because it ignores others' interests; leads to conflict; incoherent when made universal.

3️⃣ Divine Command Theory

An action is right if and only if God commands it; wrong if God forbids it. - Why non-workable / criticism: different religions command different things (no universal agreement); relies on the existence of God, so it can't persuade non-believers; the arbitrariness problem — is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it's good? If the former, morality is arbitrary.

4️⃣ Kantianism 🔴⭐ (appears in almost every case study)

  • Focuses on the action / duty, NOT the consequences.
  • You must do what is right because it is your duty (good will).
  • Kant says the only thing good without qualification is a good will.
  • Moral rules are derived through reason, expressed as the Categorical Imperative (CI) — an unconditional rule that always applies.

First Formulation — Universalizability:

"Act only on that maxim (rule) which you would want to become a universal law." - Test: imagine everyone did it. If that leads to contradiction or a world you couldn't rationally want, the action is wrong. - Example: Lying to get a loan — if everyone lied, promises would be meaningless, so the maxim self-destructs → lying is wrong.

Second Formulation — Humanity as an End (Respect Persons):

"Act so that you treat humanity, whether in yourself or others, always as an end and never merely as a means." - Test: are you using people merely as tools, without respecting their rational autonomy/consent? - IT example: Secretly collecting and selling users' data to advertisers treats users merely as a means to profit → violates the 2nd formulation → unethical.

Why workable: it explains why an action is right/wrong, applies to everyone equally, and rests on reason.

5️⃣ Utilitarianism 🔴⭐ (Principle of Utility)

  • Developed by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill.
  • A consequentialist theory: judge an action by its outcomes.
  • Principle of Utility: an action is right to the extent it increases total happiness / benefit and reduces suffering — the greatest good for the greatest number.

Act Utilitarianism — apply the Principle of Utility to each individual action. Weigh benefits vs harms of that specific act. - Method: list all who are affected, total the happiness (benefits) minus harms; if net positive → morally right. - Weaknesses: time-consuming; can justify troubling acts (e.g., sacrificing one to save many); ignores our natural sense of duty/justice.

Rule Utilitarianism — apply the Principle of Utility to moral rules. Follow the rule that, if generally adopted, produces the most overall happiness. - Example: "Don't pirate software" — as a general rule this maximizes benefit even if one specific act of piracy seems harmless.

Utilitarianism vs Kantianism (classic compare question): | | Utilitarianism | Kantianism | |---|---|---| | Judges by | Consequences/outcomes | The action itself / duty | | Key question | Does it maximize happiness? | Can the rule be universalized? Are persons respected? | | Type | Consequentialist | Deontological (duty-based) | | Weakness | Can justify sacrificing a minority | Rigid; ignores outcomes |

6️⃣ Social Contract Theory

Morality consists of the rules rational people agree to follow for mutual benefit, provided others follow them too. Associated with Hobbes, Locke, Rawls. - Rights under social contract: - Positive right — an obligation on others to provide you something (e.g., right to education, healthcare). - Negative right — an obligation on others to leave you alone / not interfere (e.g., right to free speech, privacy). - Absolute right — guaranteed without exception. - Limited right — can be restricted in some situations (e.g., free speech limited by defamation laws).

CHAPTER 2 — ISSUES PERTAINING TO NETWORK COMMUNICATION 🟡

Covers: Email & Spam, Censorship, Identity theft, Cyberbullying, Sexting.

2.1 Spam

  • Spam = the mass distribution of unsolicited messages (email is ~49% of spam; also phone calls, SMS).
  • Key attributes: bulk/mass distribution, unsolicited, often commercial, hidden or false sender information.
  • How spammers get addresses: opt-in lists, data breaches (stolen emails), harvesting from websites.
  • Methods to bypass filters: hijacking other computers (botnets) to send spam; embedding text inside images so text filters can't read it; manipulating formatting/symbols.
  • Why fighting spam is hard: spammers constantly change tactics; forge sender info; cross international borders (hard to enforce laws); cheap to send millions.

CAN-SPAM Act of 2003 (US): divides business emails into 3 categories: 1. Transactional emails (e.g., receipts, order confirmations) — legal. 2. Commercial emails (marketing) — legal if rules followed (honest subject lines, valid sender, opt-out link). 3. Unsolicited commercial email — the illegal/spam category. - Requires: no false/misleading headers or subject lines, clear opt-out mechanism, identify the message as an ad. - Applied example: "You've Won a Prize!" misleading subject → violates CAN-SPAM (deceptive subject line, no consent).

Malaysia + spam: handled mainly under the Communications and Multimedia Act (CMA) 1998; senders can be punished for sending unsolicited commercial messages / providing false sender info / no opt-out.

2.2 Internet Censorship

  • Censorship = controlling or suppressing what can be accessed/published.
  • Direct censorship types:
  • Government monopolization (state controls all media)
  • Pre-publication review (content checked before release)
  • Licensing and registration (must be approved/registered to publish)
  • Self-censorship = when people restrict themselves — this is NOT a form of direct censorship (frequent MCQ trap).
  • Why censorship is challenging/controversial: 1. It can suppress free speech / legitimate opinions authorities dislike. 2. It's applied unevenly across groups; hard to define "harmful"; global internet makes enforcement difficult; may reduce access to opposing viewpoints.

CIPA (Children's Internet Protection Act, US): schools and libraries receiving federal networking funds must filter pages containing obscenity / child pornography / harmful content.

2.3 Identity Theft

  • Identity theft = illegally accessing and using another person's personal information (to impersonate them, commit fraud).
  • Example: a hacker using stolen credit-card details to shop online; using a colleague's electronic identity to break into a company system.

2.4 Cyberbullying

  • Cyberbullying = inflicting psychological harm on another person using the internet, phone system, social media, or broadcast media (i.e., "all of the above").
  • Distinguishing feature: repeated, intentional harm through digital platforms (vs. a one-off disagreement or a strong review — those aren't cyberbullying).
  • Example: repeatedly mocking + spreading rumors about a classmate in group chats.

2.5 Sexting

  • Sexting = sending sexually suggestive text messages or emails with nude / nearly-nude photographs. Raises legal issues especially with minors.

CHAPTER 3 — LEGAL ISSUES ON IP AND PRIVACY 🔴⭐ HIGH YIELD

3.1 Intellectual Property (IP)

Intellectual property = a unique product of the human intellect that has commercial value (e.g., software, music, inventions, brand names). - 4 forms of protection: Copyright, Patent, Trademark, Trade Secret. - Not IP: a traditional dance passed down for generations (no identifiable owner / not a novel creation).

3.2 The four IP protections

Protection Protects Registration? Duration IT example
Copyright Original creative works (expression, not ideas/facts) — books, music, films, software code, art Automatic, no registration needed Author's life + years (varies by country) Source code of an app, a game's artwork
Patent Inventions — machines, processes, new & useful ideas Must apply (MyIPO in Malaysia) ~20 years, then public domain A new compression algorithm, novel hardware
Trademark Any sign representing a brand — word, logo, image (® registered, ™ unregistered) Register to get ® Indefinite if used & renewed A company logo, app name/icon
Trade Secret Confidential business info/method kept secret Not registered (kept secret) As long as it stays secret A secret ranking algorithm, a recipe formula

Key laws (Malaysia): Copyright Act 1987; Patents Act 1983; managed by MyIPO.

Copyright key facts (frequent MCQ): - Protects original creative works automatically — no registration required. - Protects the expression, NOT ideas, facts, or mathematical formulas. - Copyright holder's rights: to reproduce, distribute, perform/display in public, make derivative works. (A holder does NOT have a blanket right to "prevent all competitive works.") - FYP ownership: usually the student/creator owns it, but if created as an employee "in the course of employment," the employer typically owns it — so a university/company policy may claim ownership. Discuss both sides.

Trademark going generic: a trademark can be lost if the brand name becomes a common generic word (e.g., "escalator," "aspirin").

Service mark: like a trademark but for services (e.g., Telekom Malaysia). Product brands (Nike, Adidas 3-stripes) = trademarks.

3.3 Fair Use

Using copyrighted material without permission can be legal if it qualifies as fair use. Four factors: 1. Purpose/character of use (educational, non-profit, transformative = more likely fair; commercial = less). 2. Nature of the copyrighted work (factual vs highly creative). 3. Amount/substantiality used (a small portion vs the whole). 4. Effect on the market/value of the original (does it harm the owner's sales?).

3.4 Public Domain & Creative Commons

  • Public domain = works free for anyone to use (copyright expired, or owner released it) — no restrictions.
  • Creative Commons (CC) licenses let creators grant permissions in advance:
  • CC BY (Attribution): may use, distribute, and modify (even commercially) as long as credit is given to the creator.
  • Other variants add: NC (non-commercial), ND (no derivatives), SA (share-alike).
  • User responsibilities under CC BY: give proper attribution/credit to the original creator, indicate any changes made, don't claim it as your own, follow the specific licence terms.
  • Example (exam): "Alex used a CC BY image but didn't credit the creator and claimed it as his own." → Not appropriate — he violated the attribution requirement; must credit the creator.

3.5 Proprietary vs Open-Source Software

  • Proprietary software: source code is closed/restricted; you buy a licence to use, not modify (e.g., Microsoft Windows).
  • Open-source software: source code is openly available to view, modify, and redistribute (e.g., Linux).
  • Freeware: free to use but not necessarily open source.

3.6 Software Piracy 🔴⭐

  • Software piracy = the unauthorized copying, distribution, or use of software (violating the licence).
  • Types of piracy:
  • Softlifting — buying one licensed copy and installing it on many machines / sharing with friends.
  • Hard-disk loading — a seller installs unauthorized copies on computers being sold.
  • Counterfeiting — illegally duplicating and selling copyrighted software as if genuine.
  • Online piracy / internet piracy — downloading/distributing software over the internet illegally.
  • Renting — renting out software illegally.
  • Why unethical + harms:
  • Steals revenue → hurts developers' profits, discourages innovation, can cost jobs.
  • Users get no support/updates and risk malware from pirated copies.
  • It's a form of theft — violates the creator's property rights (ties to ACM "honor property rights").

3.7 Privacy

  • Personal information = data about an identifiable individual (name, IC number, health records, location). Public information = data openly available/meant for the public (published phone directory, company address).
  • Public record vs public information: a public record is info the government is required to maintain and make available (birth/marriage/court records); public information is any info freely available/not secret. Overlap but not identical.

Solove's Taxonomy of Privacy — 4 groups of harmful activities: 1. Information Collection (surveillance, interrogation) 2. Information Processing (aggregation, identification, insecurity, secondary use, exclusion) 3. Information Dissemination (disclosure, exposure, breach of confidentiality, blackmail, publishing someone's private emails without consent) 4. Invasion (intrusion, decisional interference) - ⚠ MCQ trap: "Information filtering" is NOT a Solove category. - Usefulness for social media: it gives a structured vocabulary to identify exactly which privacy harm a platform commits (e.g., selling profile data = secondary use/processing; leaking DMs = dissemination), helping design targeted protections.

3.8 Malaysian Data & Digital Laws (MCQ favorites)

Law Purpose

Digital Signature Act 1997 Legal recognition of digital signatures.
Electronic Commerce Act 2006 Legal recognition of electronic contracts/messages.
Communications and Multimedia Act (CMA) 1998 Regulates communications & multimedia (incl. spam, content).
Copyright Act 1987 / Patents Act 1983 IP protection.

PDPA 2010 — user/data-subject rights (know at least 2–3): 1. Right of access — to request a copy of the personal data an organization holds about you. 2. Right to correct — to correct inaccurate/incomplete data. 3. Right to withdraw consent to the processing of your data. 4. Right to prevent processing likely to cause damage or distress. 5. Right to prevent processing for direct marketing. - Violation examples: selling customer data to third parties without consent; storing unencrypted data leading to a breach; using data for a purpose users never agreed to.

If asked "why choose a trade secret over a patent?" → because a patent expires in 20 years and reveals how it works, whereas a secret can last forever (Coca-Cola formula).

3.10 ⭐ DEEP DIVE — Fair Use, worked

Fair use lets you use copyrighted material without permission in limited circumstances. Four factors, all weighed together:

Factor Favors fair use Weighs against
1. Purpose & character Education, research, criticism, parody, transformative Commercial, profit-making
2. Nature of the work Factual, published Highly creative, unpublished
3. Amount used Small portion, not the "heart" of the work The whole work, or its core
4. Effect on market No harm to sales of the original Substitutes for buying the original

Applied (past-paper question): A lecturer photocopies a journal article for class. → (1) educational, non-profit ✔ (2) factual/scholarly work ✔ (3) one article, not the whole journal ✔ (4) students wouldn't have bought the journal anyway, minimal market harm ✔ → fair use.

3.13 ⭐ DEEP DIVE — PDPA 2010, the 7 principles

Malaysia's PDPA regulates the processing of personal data in commercial transactions. Its 7 principles are a rich source of case-study marks:

  1. General Principle — must obtain consent; process only for a lawful purpose directly related to the activity.
  2. Notice and Choice Principle — must inform the data subject what data is collected, why, and who it may be disclosed to.
  3. Disclosure Principle — must not disclose data to third parties without consent, or for a purpose other than the one notified.
  4. Security Principle — must take practical steps to protect data from loss, misuse, unauthorized access (encryption, access control).
  5. Retention Principle — must not keep data longer than necessary; destroy it after.
  6. Data Integrity Principle — must keep data accurate, complete, and up to date.
  7. Access Principle — the data subject must be able to access and correct their own data.

Data-subject rights (name 2 for exam answers): right of access · right to correct · right to withdraw consent · right to prevent processing likely to cause damage/distress · right to prevent processing for direct marketing.

Applied to the HealthSecure past-paper case: sharing data with advertisers without consent breaches the Disclosure Principle and the General Principle (consent); storing unencrypted data with no access control breaches the Security Principle.

4.1 Malware (know each type — very common MCQs)

4.2 Hackers (white / grey / black hat) 🔴⭐

Hacker Who they are Permission? Intent Example
White hat Ethical security professionals ✅ With permission Improve/protect security Penetration testing a company's system under a signed contract

  • Hacker motives (differ ethically): to improve security (white hat — ethical), curiosity / expose flaws without permission (grey hat — ethically questionable because it's still unauthorized access), personal/financial gain or to cause harm (black hat — clearly unethical & illegal). The ethical difference hinges on authorization/permission and intent/harm.

4.3 Denial-of-Service (DoS) attacks

  • DoS attack = a cyberattack that floods/overwhelms a system, server, or network with traffic so it can't serve legitimate users — the goal is to disrupt availability, not steal data.
  • DDoS (Distributed DoS): launched from many machines at once (a botnet). Example: 2016 Dyn DDoS via the Mirai botnet.

4.4 Security concepts

  • Authentication = verifying who a user is.
  • Authorization = determining whether a user has permission to perform a particular action.
  • Defense: firewalls, encryption, access control, antivirus, patching, backups, staff awareness training.

4.7 ⭐ DEEP DIVE — Prevention strategies (the "recommend measures" marks)

Always split into technical and organizational — past papers explicitly ask for "technical or organizational measures."

Technical measures 1. Encryption of data at rest and in transit (directly answers PDPA Security Principle questions). 2. Access control — least privilege, role-based access, multi-factor authentication. 3. Regular patching and updates to close the security holes worms exploit. 4. Firewalls, IDS/IPS, and endpoint antivirus with automatic updates. 5. Regular offline/immutable backups, tested by restoring them — the single best ransomware defence. 6. Network segmentation so an infection can't spread laterally. 7. Email filtering to block phishing attachments and malicious links.

Organizational measures 1. Security awareness training — most malware needs a human to click something. 2. An incident response plan with defined roles, rehearsed regularly. 3. Security policies — acceptable use, BYOD, password standards, no unlicensed software (pirated software is a major malware vector). 4. Regular security audits and penetration testing by authorized (white hat) testers. 5. Vendor/third-party risk management for anyone touching your data.

4.8 ⭐ DEEP DIVE — Hacker motives and the ethics of each

Past papers ask: "Describe TWO motives that may drive a person to hack and explain how these motives differ ethically."

Motive Typical hacker Ethical assessment
Improve security / protect users White hat Ethical — authorized, consented, serves the public interest (aligns with ACM "avoid harm")
Curiosity / challenge / proving skill Grey hat Ethically questionable — no malicious intent, but unauthorized access is still a violation of consent and property rights, and testing without permission can cause accidental damage
Exposing wrongdoing / hacktivism Grey hat Contested — motive may be public interest, but the method is illegal; compare with whistleblowing criteria

The pivot for full marks: the ethical difference turns on authorization (was consent given?) and intent/harm (who benefits, who is harmed?). A white hat and a grey hat may perform the same technical act; only one had permission. CHAPTER 5 — PROFESSIONAL ETHICS 🔴⭐ HIGH YIELD

5.1 Profession vs Professional

  • Profession = a type of job requiring specialized higher education, training, and certification, governed by professional bodies, held to a code of conduct.
  • Professional = a member of a profession, trusted by society and expected to act responsibly.
  • Profession vs trade (MCQ): professions are governed by strict codes of conduct guiding moral decisions; trades generally are not.
  • Where does IT stand? Many IT roles need degrees but not all require certification/licensing — so whether IT is a "profession" in the narrow sense is debated.

Mature profession features: (1) education & entry standards, (2) certification & licensing, (3) ongoing professional development, (4) ethical standards / code of ethics.

5.2 Professional bodies

  • ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) — international; publishes the ACM Code of Ethics.
  • IEEE — international engineering/computing body.
  • MBOT (Malaysia Board of Technologists) — national body; may have regulatory authority within Malaysia's legal framework (this is what distinguishes national bodies like MBOT from international ones like ACM/IEEE — MCQ).

5.3 ACM Code of Ethics 🔴⭐ (name + explain 2 principles for the case study)

General Moral Principles — a computing professional should: 1. Contribute to society and human well-being (acknowledge all people are stakeholders). 2. Avoid harm. 3. Be honest and trustworthy. 4. Be fair and take action not to discriminate. 5. Respect the work required to produce new ideas / honor property rights (incl. copyrights, patents) — don't pirate/plagiarize. 6. Respect privacy. 7. Honor confidentiality.

  • "Respect the privacy of others" — collect only necessary data, use it only for its stated purpose, get consent, secure it, and be transparent. Behaviors that comply: not reading others' files/emails, anonymizing data, informing users what's collected, allowing users to access/correct their data.
  • Harmful actions a professional must avoid: injury/loss to users, destroying data, releasing untested/unsafe software, breaching privacy, discrimination.

Software Engineering Code of Ethics (ACM/IEEE) — key principle: "Software engineers shall act consistently with the public interest" and shall test products thoroughly. Failure-to-test example: releasing a medical device or banking app with untested code that then harms people → violates public-interest + avoid-harm duties.

5.4 Whistleblowing 🔴⭐ (appears in 8/10 papers)

  • Whistleblowing = when an employee reveals to the public or higher authority that their organization is engaged in illegal, unethical, or harmful activity.
  • Hero or traitor? Depends on motive — is it to protect the public (ethical) or for revenge/personal gain (questionable)?
  • When ethical / morally required: the wrongdoing threatens serious harm to the public and internal channels have failed (e.g., reporting a bank app released with no security that endangers customers' money).
  • When unethical / questionable: done for revenge, personal gain, or on flimsy/unverified evidence; leaking to media prematurely; motive is to avoid one's own punishment.
  • Harms whistleblowing can cause: damage to the organization's reputation, loss of coworkers' trust, the whistleblower may face retaliation/job loss, may breach confidentiality if done improperly.

De George's criteria for ethical/morally-permissible whistleblowing (adapt these as "reasons"): 1. The harm is serious and considerable to the public. 2. The employee has reported the concern to superiors first (exhausted internal channels). 3. Internal channels were exhausted without result. (For whistleblowing to be morally obligatory, De George adds:) 4. The whistleblower has documented, convincing evidence. 5. There is good reason to believe going public will prevent the harm.

Whistleblower Protection Act 2010 (Malaysia, Act 711): provides protection of the whistleblower's identity/confidentiality and safeguards against retaliation. It does NOT give complete immunity for all disclosures, does not reward all reports, and does not permit releasing trade secrets to the media.

5.7 ⭐ DEEP DIVE — Whistleblowing, complete

Definition: an employee (or former employee) discloses to the public or to a higher authority that their organization is engaged in illegal, unethical, immoral, or harmful activity.

Types: Internal (report up the chain of command) vs External (to regulators, press, public). Open (named) vs Anonymous.

De George's criteria — memorize as 3 + 2:

Whistleblowing is morally PERMISSIBLE when: 1. The organization's action will cause serious and considerable harm to the public. 2. The employee has reported the concern to their immediate superior and made their moral concern known. 3. The employee has exhausted internal channels (gone up the chain) without result.

Whistleblowing becomes morally OBLIGATORY when, in addition: 4. The whistleblower has documented, convincing evidence a reasonable observer would accept. 5. There is good reason to believe going public will prevent the harm (a realistic chance of success).

Motives — the "hero or traitor" question: | Good motives (→ ethical) | Questionable motives (→ unethical) | |---|---| | Protecting public safety | Revenge against a manager | | Preventing serious harm | Personal financial gain / reward-seeking | | Acting from ethical duty | Avoiding one's own punishment | | Exposing corruption | Seeking attention or publicity |

Harms whistleblowing can cause: damage to the organization's reputation (possibly unfairly, if the claim is wrong); job losses for innocent colleagues; loss of trust within the team; the whistleblower risks retaliation, career damage, legal action and personal stress; may breach legitimate confidentiality obligations.

Whistleblower Protection Act 2010 (Act 711, Malaysia) provides: - ✅ Protection of the whistleblower's identity (confidentiality) - ✅ Immunity from civil/criminal action for the disclosure itself - ✅ Protection against detrimental action / retaliation (dismissal, demotion, harassment) - ❌ NOT complete immunity for all disclosures; ❌ NOT rewards for all reports; ❌ NOT permission to release trade secrets to the media; ❌ protection can be revoked if the disclosure is made knowing it's false, or if the whistleblower participated in the wrongdoing.

Model answer skeleton (worth memorizing):

"Whistleblowing is when an employee reveals illegal or unethical organizational conduct to a higher authority or the public. It is ethical when serious public harm is at stake and internal channels have been exhausted — for example, an engineer reporting that a banking app was released without security testing, exposing customers' funds. It is unethical/questionable when driven by revenge or personal gain, or based on unverified claims — for example, leaking confidential documents to the press to damage a manager after being denied promotion. De George requires serious harm, prior internal reporting, and exhausted internal channels; for it to be obligatory, documented evidence and a realistic chance of preventing the harm are also needed."

CHAPTER 6 — ETHICAL IMPACT OF IT ON WORK AND SOCIETY 🟠

Four themes: Automation, Telework, Globalization, Digital Divide.

6.1 Automation

  • Automation = using technology/software to perform tasks with little or no human involvement.
  • Ethical issue: job displacement — automation can replace human workers (esp. repetitive jobs), raising fairness concerns. Also bias in automated decisions, and over-surveillance of the remaining workers.
  • Case: Amazon warehouses use robots + monitor workers for speed (some fired by algorithm).
  • Ethical actions a company should take when automating (e.g., replacing cashiers): 1. Reskill / retrain affected workers and redeploy them to new roles. 2. Give advance notice, fair severance, and transition support. 3. Phase in gradually; involve workers in the change; be transparent.

6.2 Telework (Telecommuting / Remote / WFH) 🔴⭐ (appears in 7/10)

  • Telework = working outside the traditional office using digital tools (often from home). Also: remote work, telecommuting; hybrid = mix of office + remote.
  • Benefits (employees): flexible schedule + better work-life balance; no commute; personalized productivity; can work from anywhere; saves cost.
  • Challenges/risks: blurred work/personal boundaries → overwork/burnout; isolation / lack of teamwork; data & communication security risks; longer hours for same pay; hard to monitor performance fairly.
  • Employer ethical responsibilities: respect employee privacy (don't over-monitor); ensure fair workload & evaluation; provide proper tools/security; avoid an "always-online" culture; trust employees.
  • Employee ethical responsibilities: be honest about hours/productivity; protect company data & confidentiality; stay reachable/accountable during work hours; avoid misusing company resources.

6.3 Globalization

  • Globalization = the process of interaction and integration among people, companies, and governments of different nations, driven by international trade & investment and aided by information technology.
  • Ethical issues: exploiting weaker labor laws in developing nations; avoiding health/safety or environmental regulations by relocating to countries with weaker laws; unequal pay; outsourcing that removes local jobs; unfair conditions for poorer countries.

6.4 Digital Divide

  • Digital divide = the gap between those who have access to ICT (computers, internet, skills) and those who do not.
  • Global digital divide = the same gap between countries (developed vs developing nations).
  • Social divide appears between: urban vs rural, younger vs older, educated vs less-educated, rich vs poor (i.e., "all of the above").
  • Example: a rural student can't attend online class due to no internet access.
  • How to close the gap: provide affordable internet access + infrastructure; basic IT/digital-literacy training; low-cost devices; low-bandwidth, multilingual content for inclusivity; government/NGO subsidies.

6.5 Employee / Workplace Monitoring

  • Employers monitor internet use, emails, keystrokes, location, productivity.
  • Advantages: security, productivity, prevents misuse, legal compliance.
  • Disadvantages/ethical concerns: invades privacy, erodes trust, causes stress, may be excessive/constant surveillance.
  • Kantian objection: secret/constant surveillance treats employees merely as a means (to productivity), not as ends deserving respect → unethical (a favorite exam application).

6.6 Workplace well-being (recent addition)

  • IT professionals can promote well-being by designing tools that allow screen breaks, mental-health monitoring, reasonable notifications, and healthy work patterns — rather than an always-on, high-pressure culture.

PART 5 — ANSWER FRAMEWORKS + MCQ BANK

5.1 Master framework for the CASE STUDY (Section C)

When you see any AI/data scenario, structure each part like this:

(a) Utilitarianism:

"Utilitarianism judges an action by whether it maximizes total happiness for the greatest number. Benefits: [company profit, efficiency, some users helped]. Harms: [harm to many users — privacy loss, psychological harm, discrimination, breach affecting thousands]. Because the harms fall on a large number of people and outweigh the benefits that accrue mainly to the company, the action does NOT maximize overall happiness → under (act) utilitarianism it is ethically unjustified."

(b) Kantian ethics:

First Formulation (universalizability): if every company [deceived users / sold data without consent / deployed biased AI] as a universal rule, trust would collapse and the practice self-defeats → the maxim can't be universalized → wrong. (c) Two ACM Code principles violated: pick 2 and explain: - Avoid harm — the system caused psychological/financial/discriminatory harm. - Respect privacy — data collected/shared without proper consent. - Be fair and not discriminate — biased outcomes against women/minorities. - Be honest and trustworthy — company misled users about data use / safety. - Honor property rights — if code was reused without a licence.

(d) Your opinion / recommendation: take a clear stance + justify with professional responsibility + fairness:

audit & fix the algorithm; obtain explicit informed consent; encrypt data + add access control; be transparent; retrain the model on unbiased data; add human oversight & accountability; comply with PDPA 2010. Conclude that professional duty and fairness require correcting the harm even at a cost to short-term profit.

  1. Define: an employee revealing illegal/unethical/harmful organizational activity to authority or the public.

6.2 The Seven-Theory Engine

This is the transferable skill. For any case, each theory asks one question. Memorize the question, and the verdict follows.

# Theory The ONE question it asks Typical verdict on corporate misconduct
1 Ethical Egoism Did the actor benefit themselves? ✅ Usually says RIGHT (company profited) — this is the theory that defends the wrongdoer
2 Act Utilitarianism Did this specific act produce more total happiness than harm? ❌ Usually WRONG (harm to thousands > profit to one firm)
3 Rule Utilitarianism If everyone followed this as a rule, would society be better off? ❌ WRONG ("companies may sell data without consent" as a universal rule destroys trust)
4 Kantianism Can the maxim be universalized? Are people treated as ends, not merely means? ❌ WRONG (deception can't universalize; users used as means to profit)
5 Social Contract Were the rights people agreed to (privacy, consent, fair treatment) honored? ❌ WRONG (violated the implicit agreement + negative right to privacy)
6 Relativism Does the individual/culture involved consider it acceptable? 🤷 NO CLEAR ANSWER — this is the point; it's non-workable
7 Divine Command Does a religious/higher law forbid it (theft, deceit, harming others)? ❌ WRONG, but only persuasive to believers

The two theories students always get wrong

Ethical Egoism is not "being selfish is bad." Under egoism the action is morally RIGHT if it served the actor's self-interest. Your job is to state that verdict honestly, then show its weakness (it ignores everyone else and collapses when universalized). Examiners give marks for applying the theory correctly, not for agreeing with it.

Act vs Rule Utilitarianism must give you two different analyses. If you write the same paragraph twice, you lose marks. - Act: tally benefits and harms of this one incident. → "The breach harmed 200,000 users; profit went to one firm. Net harm. Wrong." - Rule: evaluate the general rule behind it. → "If the rule 'firms may store data unencrypted to save cost' were universally adopted, breaches would be routine and digital commerce would collapse. Wrong."

6.5 The 6-step drill (do this on your assignment case, then you can do it on any exam scenario)

  1. State the act in one neutral sentence. "Company X stored 200,000 users' health records unencrypted and shared them with advertisers without consent." No adjectives — examiners deduct for emotive framing instead of analysis.
  2. List stakeholders in two columns: who is harmed / who benefits. This single table feeds every subsequent theory.
  3. Run the seven questions from 6.2. Write the verdict before the justification, so your logic can't wander.
  4. Note where theories clash. Egoism vs Kant is the sharpest clash and the easiest 5 marks in a comparison question.
  5. Name the ACM principle(s) breached — avoid harm, respect privacy, be honest, be fair/don't discriminate, honor property rights.
  6. Deliver a verdict as an IT professional, not as a private opinion. The magic phrase: "As a computing professional bound by the ACM Code, my duty to avoid harm and respect privacy outweighs the employer's commercial interest; therefore…"

6.6 Exam-condition timing for Section C (20 marks / ~30 min)

  • ~3 min: read the scenario twice, underline the harm, the consent failure, and any stolen/copied asset.
  • ~6 min per 5-mark theory part: 1 line stating the theory's rule → 3–4 lines applying it to named facts → 1 line verdict. Marks come from naming specific facts from the scenario, not from restating the theory.
  • ~6 min: ACM principles — name it, quote the gist, tie to a fact.
  • ~5 min: recommendation — 3 concrete, actionable measures + a professional-duty justification.
