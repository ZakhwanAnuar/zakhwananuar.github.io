Exam Format & Strategy

Across every past paper from 2019 to 2025 the structure is almost identical. Knowing the shape of the paper is half the battle — you can budget your time before you even read a question.

A
20 MCQ · 20 marks · one best answer
B
10–20 True/False · 10–20 marks
C
3 structured questions · 60–70 marks
33
typical total questions (43 in older papers)

The three sections, decoded

SectionWhat it isMarksWhere you answer
A — MCQ20 multiple-choice, "choose the BEST answer". Heavy on definitions, theorists, and matching tools to problem types.20OMR form or answer booklet (newer papers)
B — True / FalseStatements you mark T or F. Almost every one is a deliberate "swap-a-word" trap (e.g. divergent ↔ convergent).10–20OMR form / answer booklet
C — Short / structured3 questions built around tables you copy out. Always: one Forced-Connection or Creative-Press task, one SCAMPER task, one DATT problem-solving task.60–70Answer booklet
Time budget that works Section C is worth ~70% of marks but rewards structure over prose. Spend ~25 min on A+B combined (≈45 sec/question — don't overthink; your first instinct on a definition is usually right), then ~40 min per Section C question. Draw the table first, fill the easy rows, then justify. Leave 10 min to write every Final Recommendation — students routinely run out of time and drop those 5–6 easy marks.
Golden rule for Section C Every Section C answer is graded on the same three moves: (1) a correct idea/innovation, (2) a justification tied to a named concept from the syllabus (value creation / N.A.S.A., a Dacey quality, a creative-person trait), and (3) a closing Final Recommendation. Never give an idea without naming the concept it demonstrates.

U1

Defining Creativity

Creativity is treated as an open, infinite, limitless concept — but the course insists you can still define, measure, and above all create value with it. This unit supplies the definitions that Section A returns to again and again.

1.1 Creativity as a limitless concept

Because creativity is subjective and depends on perspective and standards of expectation, the course gives you 4 models to measure it. Each model answers a different purpose, and each maps onto one element of value creation (N.A.S.A.).

ModelWhoMeasures how creative a…N.A.S.A. element
Csikszentmihalyi Systems ModelMihalyi Csikszentmihalyi…piece of work is within its social setting (artist ↔ domain ↔ field)Attractive
Taxonomy of Creative DesignsNilsson (2011)…work is by degree of originalityNovel
Guilford MeasuresGuilford & Torrance…person is, via divergent thinkingAppropriate
Requirements Model—…work is against a field/programme using strict expert criteriaSocial impact
Value creation = N.A.S.A. Novel (original, new, unexpected) · Appropriate (useful, fitting, better way of doing things) · Social impact (accepted by community, solves problems, improves economy) · Attractive (aesthetic sensibility — pleasing, evokes emotion). An innovation must serve a good purpose for its target audience. It does NOT have to be cheap, technologically advanced, or accepted by everyone.

Nilsson's Taxonomy — the degrees of originality (low → high)

DegreeWhat it does
ImitationCopies a product to add to the market.
VariationRe-designs a product by changing it slightly (e.g. new colour).
CombinationCombines two products to produce a new one.
TransformationReconstructs a product to work differently for the same purpose.
Original / Innovation ← highestProduces something completely new and original.

1.3 Simple & complex definitions

Simple (Yew, 2007)

Creativity is fundamentally about making connections. Classic examples: the wine press + coin punch → Gutenberg's printing press; a car's petrol engine + boat → Ole Evinrude's outboard motor. Connections can be extended (touch-'n-go, drive-thru, Grab).

Complex (Torrance, 1974)

A 4-part process: (1) become sensitive to problems/gaps — identify the problem; (2) search for solutions, form & test hypotheses; (3) modify & retest; (4) communicate the results. Boil it down to: Identify → Solve → Share.

Definition cross-check Sternberg: "new and useful products." · Lubart: "novel and appropriate." · Ken Robinson: "the process of having original ideas that have value." · de Bono: "finding better ways of doing things." A creative idea is always logical in hindsight — otherwise it's just "crazy."

1.4 The Four-C Model (Kaufman & Beghetto, 2009)

Big-C (genius C)
Eminent, history-making creativity (Einstein, Hawking).
Pro-C (expert C)
Professional-level expertise.
little-c (personal c)
Everyday creative expression.
mini-c (everyday c)
Solving small everyday problems — creativity you do without realising it.
Take-away Everyone has creative potential; it can be learned and trained. Value creation is dynamic: industrial-era mass production → today's innovation of materials/processes → future economies of creativity (mass customisation).
U2

Elements of Creative Thinking

Rhodes' (1961) framework — the 4 Ps — plus Dacey's 8 qualities of the creative mind. The 8 qualities are doubly important: they turn up in Section A/B and you must name them to justify Section C answers.

2.1 The 4 Ps of creativity (Rhodes, 1961)

① Creative Person

Traits: imaginative, flexible, curious, willing to try new ideas. Studied via Multiple Intelligences (Gardner) and the Big Five / OCEAN.

② Creative Press / Place

The environment: classroom, office, study space, and the people & life experiences (teachers, peers, parents, society, hobbies, travel) that nurture creativity.

③ Creative Product

What makes an idea creative — making connections between concepts, fields of knowledge, and life experiences (e.g. aerogel, hydrophobic & self-healing materials → new uses).

④ Creative Process

The 5 stages that generate ideas: Knowledge → Thinking → Incubation → Illumination → Development (covered in Unit 3).

Gardner's Multiple Intelligences — know the odd-ones-out for T/F

IntelligenceTypical people
Linguisticauthors, journalists, poets
Logical / Mathematicalengineers, scientists, economists, detectives (people who work with computers)
Musicalcomposers, singers, pianists
Visual / Spatialinterior decorators, designers, architects
Bodily-Kinestheticsportspeople, dancers
Intrapersonalphilosophers, counsellors — understanding one's own feelings (NOT communicating with others)
Interpersonalteachers, facilitators, politicians, salespeople — communicating with others
Naturalisticfarmers, botanists, environmentalists
Existential (added 2020)religious leaders, philosophers, bloggers
Big Five (OCEAN) trap Openness · Conscientiousness · Extraversion · Agreeableness · Neuroticism. Section B loves misdefining agreeableness (which means compassionate/cooperative/kind) as "outgoing" (extraversion), "emotional" (neuroticism), or "disciplined/hardworking" (conscientiousness). All such statements are FALSE.

2.2 Is creativity born or made?

de Bono's answer "Creative thinking is NOT a talent; it is a skill that can be learned." It strengthens natural abilities and improves teamwork, productivity and profits.

2.3 The 8 Qualities of the Creative Mind (Dacey, 1989)

Memorise these — they are your Section C justification vocabulary.

QualityMeaning
1. Tolerance for ambiguityNeeds a greater degree of uncertainty before feeling fear; ambiguity raises curiosity and drives them out of the comfort zone.
2. Stimulus freedomBends the rules to their needs; doesn't assume rules exist when a situation is ambiguous (vs "stimulus bound" = follows rules rigidly).
3. Functional freedomSees unconventional uses for things (vs "functional fixity" = accepting things as unchangeable, which blocks ideas).
4. FlexibilityGenerates ideas across many different categories.
5. Risk-takingWilling to take risks; people who aim low are less creative.
6. Preference for disorderPrefers complex/asymmetrical figures — the Barron-Welsh Figure Preference Test.
7. Delayed gratificationEndures prolonged effort for higher rewards (Edison's 2,004 filament experiments).
8. AndrogynyUses both male & female characteristics as appropriate — NOT equal hormone levels. "Creativity requires both sensitivity and independence" (Torrance).
Two flexibility tests to remember TTCT (Torrance Test of Creative Thinking) scores 4 scales: Fluency (many ideas), Flexibility (different categories), Originality (rare ideas), Elaboration (well-described). The Five-Circles class activity is a TTCT-style test of divergent thinking.
U3

Creative Process & Thinking

The 5-stage creative process is the source of the "Sarah / Ahmad / Maryam" scenario questions. Get the boundary between Knowledge and Incubation right and you'll ace them.

3.1 Creative potentials — Maslow

The 6/6 question Self-Actualisation (Maslow, 1943): "What a man can be, he must be." It is the idea that a person should maximise his potentials / natural talents to be what he is able to be — the top of Maslow's hierarchy. Not about shelter, belonging, or earning a living. This exact phrasing is the safest mark on the paper.

3.2 Creativity & adulthood — George Land's NASA test

Land (1968) gave NASA's divergent-thinking test to 1,600 children and re-tested them over time. The share scoring at "genius" level collapses with age:

5 yrs
98%
10 yrs
30%
15 yrs
12%
Adults
2%

Creativity diminishes with age because of judgement, criticism, censoring and stress ("the greatest obstacles"). School over-emphasises content subjects and under-emphasises creative subjects. Ken Robinson agrees: "people are educated out of their creativity" because mistakes are condemned.

3.3 The Creative Process — 5 stages (Yew, 2007)

#StageWhat happensScenario cue
0Identify the problemBecome sensitive to the gap/need (Torrance)."given a task to increase sales"
1KnowledgeActively gather info — formal & experiential learning; research, read, talk to people, explore what you're curious about."reads about consumer behaviour / talks to people to research"
2ThinkingWork the problem using lateral & vertical thinking."thinks hard at her workstation"
3IncubationTake a break / do something else. Mind enters Default Mode Network (DMN); subconscious makes new connections; happy hormones (dopamine) released."leaves workstation, coffee break, chats & laughs"
4Illumination (Eureka!)The "a-ha!" lightbulb moment — the idea strikes."sees a boy with a lollipop — an idea strikes"
5DevelopmentHardest stage: act on the idea to make it useful, functional, profitable — proposals, approvals, appointing agencies, months of work."prepared a proposal, worked with Procurement, took months"
Knowledge vs Incubation — the exam's trap If the character is actively researching (reading about consumer behaviour, browsing for information) → Knowledge. If they've stopped working and are relaxing (coffee, chatting, laughing, doing something unrelated) → Incubation. Both look like "left the workstation," so read what they're actually doing.

Lateral (de Bono)Vertical
Generative · concerned with richnessSelective · concerned with rightness

Breaking patterns (Unit 3b, de Bono) The mind is a pattern-making system; lateral thinking restructures patterns. Three techniques: (1) Stepping stone — question/challenge ("what if… why… how…?"); (2) Reverse — reverse the normal; (3) Random word — innovate by connecting to a random item (design a skateboard via a "shark"). "Po" is de Bono's idea-generation / provocation tool.
U4

Idea Generation: Brainstorming & Mind Mapping

Two idea-generation techniques whose rules are the exam target — especially "emphasis on quantity" and "no criticism."

4.1 Brainstorming

A "storm in the brain" — letting ideas free-flow without worrying about errors, logic, or rules.

5 basic guidelines

  1. No criticising ideas
  2. No evaluation of ideas
  3. Focus on quantity of ideas
  4. Encourage wild ideas
  5. Cross-fertilise ideas

Steps

  1. Define the problem
  2. Members branch ideas from the central idea
  3. Share on a screen for all
  4. Make time for incubation
  5. Revisit & then organise / cluster / cross-fertilise
  6. Select workable ideas
The one-word MCQ "During brainstorming/mind mapping there should be ___ of ideas" → emphasis on quantity and no criticism. NOT quality, NOT sorting, NOT "no writing down."

4.2 Mind Mapping (Tony Buzan)

Used to start or to organise brainstorming — clustering ideas into groups/subgroups. Our brain looks for patterns & completion (Gestalt theory); a mind map satisfies this and builds a conceptual framework. More branching = more advanced thinking.

Buzan's 7 mind-map characteristics

  1. Start branching from the centre of the page
  2. Use an image/symbol for the central idea
  3. Use vibrant colours
  4. Connect central image → main branches → 2nd, 3rd levels
  5. Use curved lines, not straight
  6. One keyword per heading, not sentences
  7. Use more illustrations than lengthy words
Brainstorm vs Mind map Brainstorming = free flow, focus on number of ideas, no judgement. Mind mapping = organising ideas into directions/branches, where you select what to develop.
U5

Forced Connection & SCAMPER

The two engines behind Section C Questions 1 & 2. Master the mechanics: how you turn a random item or an object into concrete, justified innovations.

5.1 Forced Connection

An idea-generation tool that puts two seemingly unrelated concepts together — the essence is randomness: A + B = C. Take an idea and force it against an unrelated one to see what emerges (pet turtle + pager; wine press + coin punch → printing press).

The 5-step method (this is exactly the Section C table)

  1. Identify the problem of an existing design (e.g. long supermarket queue).
  2. Pick a random item to draw from (e.g. a backpack).
  3. List the characteristics of the random item.
  4. Transfer / adopt each characteristic into the existing design.
  5. Innovate a new idea for each.
Worked micro-example (backpack → supermarket queue) Characteristic: a backpack has shoulder straps for comfort → Solution: provide benches so customers queue comfortably seated. Characteristic: a backpack has many compartments → Solution: multiple express lanes sorted by basket size. Notice the pattern: 1 mark for the characteristic, 2 marks for the solution.

5.2 SCAMPER (Osborn 1953 · Eberle 1971)

A mnemonic of "idea-spurring questions" that forces you to think about an object in 7 distinct ways. This is the Section C Q2 backbone every year.

S
Substitute
What can I replace/swap? (digital clock replaces analog)
C
Combine
What can I merge/integrate? (library + café; salon + café)
A
Adapt
What can I borrow from another idea? (taxi → Grab; airport carousel → sushi carousel)
M
Modify / Magnify / Minify
What can I stretch, exaggerate or distort? (small → large aquarium)
P
Put to other use
What else could this be used for? (fridge → a door)
E
Eliminate
What part can I remove? (cordless kettle, bladeless fan, zipper-less jacket)
R

What if I turn it inside out / transform the way it's done? (zoo → wildlife safari)
Name-the-stage examples (memorise these) Rocking horse ride = Put to other use · Digital vs analog clock = Substitute · Stretchable jacket with no zippers/buttons (through a neck hole) = Eliminate · House styled like a holiday resort = Adapt · Floor fan mounted on a poster/flower stand = Combine.

5.3 Innovation & Creative Adaptations

Adaptations are the most common way to innovate — ideas are borrowed and changed to suit new functions. Two types:

Technological adaptation
Transfer an existing technology to new uses (the "intelligent parking chair" → Pro-Pilot chair).
Nature-inspired adaptation — Biomimicry
Copy nature: Japan's Shinkansen (kingfisher birds), the UltraCane (bats' echolocation), the Eastgate Centre (termite mounds' self-cooling).
Summary Forced Connection forces you to apply one item's characteristics to another. SCAMPER forces you to innovate one item in 7 different ways. Impactful innovation rests on careful research and sound knowledge.
U6

de Bono's Thinking Tools: PMI & OPV

Edward de Bono coined "lateral thinking" and created the Direct Attention Thinking Tools (DATT). The whole exam hinges on knowing which tool fits which problem — the single most tested distinction in Section A and B.

The DATT matching table — learn this cold PMI → problems with advantages & disadvantages of an action. OPV → problems involving different groups of people's views. C&S → problems needing time frames / forecasting. CAF → decisions needing a complete list of considerations.

6.1 PMI — Plus · Minus · Interesting

Forces objectivity: instead of defending an opinion you already hold (bias), you weigh both sides plus the neutral-but-notable points. It is not a yes/no decision tool — it's for accepting an idea after considering both sides.

PlusMinusInteresting
The positive/good points.The negative/bad points.Points that are neither plus nor minus — worth exploring, potential for future progress, "it would be interesting if…".

Suitable for: "Should we ban handphones for under-5s?", "Should final exams be abolished?" — issues with clear pros and cons. Method: pick issue → brainstorm solutions → select → analyse each through P, M, I → Final Recommendation.

6.2 OPV — Other People's Views

Looking at an issue from other people's standpoints. Identify everyone involved, put yourself in their shoes, and consider how each group thinks, feels and responds — giving a wider perspective.

Suitable for: "How can we control sugar use in F&B?", "Consequences of higher electricity tariffs?", "What if we had a 4-day work week?" — issues affecting distinct groups (schools, parents, businesses, government). Often combined with PMI: run each group's view through Plus/Minus/Interesting.

The recurring True/False trap "OPV allows us to think through problems using time frames" = FALSE (that's C&S). "OPV allows us to think using factors that affect a decision" = FALSE (that's CAF). OPV is only about viewpoints of different people.
U7

de Bono's Thinking Tools: C&S & CAF

The two DATT tools you'll most likely build a Section C table with: C&S structures answers by time, CAF structures them by options.

7.1 C&S — Consequences & Sequel

Purpose: to assess a situation before acting and to forecast consequences. It requires thinking in time frames — so it's speculative ("may be", "could be") and helps you make calculated plans.

Time frameRange (as taught)
ImmediateNow → end of this year
Short term1–5 years
Medium term6–10 years
Long termBeyond 10 years

Suitable for: "How do we develop a sustainable student-entrepreneurship programme?", "How do we increase autism awareness?" — anything needing planning from now into the future. The solution you propose must suit each time frame.

7.2 CAF — Consider All Factors

Purpose: to avoid pitfalls before making a decision by asking "What factor has been left out? What else must we consider?" More complete information → better thinking → a calculated decision. Always consult a good source of information.

Suitable for: choosing among options — "Which job offer?", "Which tablet to buy?", "Which university abroad?" — where you list factors (fees, course, accommodation, food, transport, academic standing…) and weigh options against them.

Combining tools (very common in Section C Q3) Real papers ask you to combine: C&S + CAF (a long-term programme, listing factors at each stage), C&S + PMI (forecast each time frame, then weigh P/M/I), or OPV + PMI / OPV + C&S. Build the table with the time frames or groups down the side and the second tool's columns across the top.
U8

Six Thinking Hats

de Bono's tool for thinking through a problem one mode at a time — so a group's thinking runs in parallel instead of mixing logic and feelings all at once. You must know each colour, its symbol, and its mode.

Blue

= Sky · conductor

Big picture, overview, main goal & objectives. Usually where discussion starts.

White

= Blank paper

Pure facts — data, reports, figures, research, case studies.

Green

= New leaves

New ideas, fresh approaches, alternative solutions, creativity.

Yellow

= Sun

Positive thinking — benefits, upside, how it may work.

Red

= Fire / blood

Emotions — feelings, intuition, reactions, opinions.

Black

= Judge's robe

Critical evaluation — caution, judging, negatives, analytical arguments.

Pairing traps in Section A The correct pairs are strict: Blue = overview, White = facts, Green = new ideas, Yellow = positive, Red = emotions/reactions, Black = critical evaluation. Wrong-answer options swap them — e.g. "white = positive side" (✗, white is facts), "green = the facts" (✗, green is new ideas), "black = research real facts" (✗, black is critical), "red = overview" (✗, red is emotion).
Best-use True/False Six Thinking Hats is BEST used to look at a solution from various modes of thinking (TRUE). If a statement says "from various groups of people's perspectives," that describes OPV, so it's FALSE. Focused single-mode thinking makes the discussion organised and efficient — it is not primarily for "generating new ideas" (that's just the green hat).

Applying the hats (the Unit 8 robot/AI walkthrough)

A typical run on "should we adopt humanoid robots?": Blue — what's the main impact & which sector benefits? → White — latest developments, costs, what it can/can't do → Yellow — benefits & problems it solves → Green — fresh applications, combining with other AI → Red — people's reactions (employers, employees, end-users) → Black — how practical/suitable in our local context, expert cautions → Final Recommendation.

A

MCQ Answer Bank

The exact recurring Section A questions, reworded, with the correct answer and the reasoning. If you can answer these on sight, Section A is 18+/20.

Maslow's "Self-Actualisation" essentially means a person should…
✓ maximise potentials to be what he is able to be Not belonging, shelter, or earning a living — it's the top of the hierarchy.
The simplest definition of creative thinking (Yew, 2007) is to…
✓ make connections Creativity = making new connections between existing things.
For an innovation to be "value creation" it must do all of the following EXCEPT…
✓ be cheap / be accepted by everyone Value creation (N.A.S.A.) requires novelty, appropriateness, social impact, attractiveness — not low price or universal acceptance.
Nilsson's Taxonomy — the HIGHEST degree of creativity is to…
✓ produce something completely new and original Order low→high: imitation, variation, combination, transformation, original.
The Five-Circles / The Island / many-uses-of-a-mirror activity is mainly a test of…
✓ divergent thinking Assessed via the TTCT; flexibility of ideas.
She leaves her workstation, goes for a coffee break, chats and laughs → which stage?
✓ Incubation Taking a break, DMN active, subconscious making connections. (If she were reading/researching → Knowledge.)
Watching a passer-by, an idea suddenly strikes → which stage?
✓ Illumination The eureka / a-ha! moment.
She prepares a proposal, gets approvals, appoints an agency over months → which stage?
✓ Development Acting on the idea to make it useful, functional, profitable — the hardest stage.
During brainstorming / mind mapping there should be ___ of ideas.
✓ emphasis on quantity (and no criticism) Never quality, sorting, or "no writing down."
A digital clock replacing an analog clock is an example of ___ in SCAMPER.
✓ Substitute A rocking horse ride = Put to other use; a stretch jacket with no zips = Eliminate.
de Bono's ___ can BEST analyse problems that have advantages & disadvantages of an action.
✓ PMI C&S = time frames · CAF = considerations when planning · OPV = views of different groups.
The problem best solved by PMI is…
✓ "Should we allow X?" & "advantages/disadvantages of X" Yes/no & pros/cons questions. "What stages over 20 years?" → C&S. "How do we educate different groups?" → OPV.
Higher creativity is BEST achieved by…
✓ creating activities + diverse friends + travel NOT being organised, disciplined, or following a routine.
The brain enters Default Mode Network (DMN) when…
✓ doing habitual actions / mind wandering Washing dishes, dressing, mind drifting to the future. NOT when brainstorming a new design or doing maths.
Forced Connection involves…
✓ designing a new product using characteristics of an unrelated item Essence = randomness (A + B = C).
Correct Six Thinking Hats pairing…
✓ Blue = overview · Yellow = positive White = facts, Green = new ideas, Red = emotions, Black = critical.
The Barron-Welsh test shows creative people prefer complex/asymmetrical figures, suggesting they…
✓ are risk-takers & like the unpredictable/interesting Preference for disorder (a Dacey quality).
George Land's "greatest obstacles" to creative potential are…
✓ judgement, criticism, censoring, stress (all) Schooling over-emphasises content, under-emphasises creative subjects.

True/False Traps

Every statement below is a real recurring pattern. The answer is given as T or F with the reason. Watch for the swapped word — that's the whole game.

Choosing the most appropriate final design from many ideas is "convergent thinking."
T Choosing the best = convergent. If it says "divergent" or "lateral" → F.
The brain is more creative when one is relaxed / less alert.
T DMN & incubation. If it says "on alert" → F.
Not being tied down by existing designs = stimulus freedom.
T And being tied down = functional fixity (also T when stated that way).
A high "agreeableness" score means you are outgoing / emotional / disciplined.
F Trap. Agreeableness = compassionate & cooperative. Outgoing = extraversion, emotional = neuroticism, disciplined = conscientiousness.
Creative potential is reduced when surrounded by negative people.
T But "increased when surrounded by people who think like you" → F (need diversity).
Androgyny = using either/both male & female characteristics in appropriate situations.
T "Equal amount of male/female hormones" → F.
OPV lets us think through problems using time frames.
F That's C&S. OPV = different people's viewpoints. "Using factors that affect a decision" is also F (that's CAF).
Six Thinking Hats is best for looking at a solution from various groups of people's perspectives.
F That's OPV. Six Hats = various modes of thinking (that version is T).
Intrapersonal intelligence is the ability to communicate effectively with others.
F That's interpersonal. Intrapersonal = understanding your own feelings.
Illumination / incubation is the process of obtaining a patent for your innovation.
F Illumination = eureka moment; patents belong to Development.
Dopamine is released when you take a break and solve a nagging problem.
T But "released when you think non-stop for days" → F.
de Bono is a proponent of vertical thinking.
F He champions lateral / parallel thinking. "Po" is his idea-generation tool (T).
Knowledge is the raw material of idea generation; consulting experts is part of the Knowledge stage.
T But "understanding of do's & don'ts is the starting point of creative ideas" → F (rules restrict).
The Wright Brothers (told men can't fly, but flew) demonstrated stimulus freedom.
T They didn't accept the "rule."
Creating an appropriate, useful, practical innovation needs both lateral & vertical (intuition & analytic) thinking.
T Divergent to generate, convergent to select.

Always finish with a FINAL RECOMMENDATION (5–6 marks) Use this 3-part skeleton every time: (1) Background of the problem in one line → (2) The proposed main solutions are… (list your 2–3 best) → (3) However, considerations to take are… (list the key CAF/Minus points). This structure alone scores the recommendation marks even when you're short on time.
The OPV + PMI variant When the prompt names groups (schools, parents, hospitals, social media, manufacturers), put groups across the top and run each through Plus / Minus / Interesting down the side — or list a solution per group per time frame. Same finish: Final Recommendation.

Thinking pairs — don't mix them up

  • Lateral = Divergent = many ideas = creativity = right brain = "what's possible?"
  • Vertical = Convergent = one best answer = intelligence = left brain = "what's right?"

Free your thinking (Dacey freedoms)

  • Stimulus freedom ↔ stimulus bound (rules)
  • Functional freedom ↔ functional fixity
  • Both let you escape the "box" of your mind.
