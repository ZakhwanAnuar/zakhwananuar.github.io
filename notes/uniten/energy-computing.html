The exam, decoded

The paper is always 100 marks over 2h30m in three sections. The mark split moves around a little each semester — here's exactly how, so nothing surprises you on the day.

Section A

Multiple Choice

10–20 questions, 15–30 marks. Fast, factual, definition-heavy. Draws evenly from all four chapters. This is where you bank easy marks — the same ~20 facts recur across papers.

Section B

Discussion / Terms

4–10 questions, 30–60 marks — the biggest section. "Explain / describe / compare + give TWO examples." Structured, points-based marking. Master the recurring templates and you own this section.

Section C

Case Study / Essay

1 long scenario, 20–30 marks, with 3–4 sub-questions. Almost always Malaysia + sustainability/digital transformation. You apply the notes to a passage you read on the spot.

Introduction to Energy & Computing
Forms of energy · measurement · sources · Malaysia's landscape · digitalisation
+

What energy is

Energy — the capacity to do work or produce heat. It exists in many forms and can be transformed between them but cannot be created or destroyed (First Law of Thermodynamics).
  • Power = rate of energy transfer (watts). Capacity = maximum power output. Efficiency = useful output ÷ input energy × 100%.
  • All conversions lose some energy as heat — the Second Law of Thermodynamics.

Forms of energy

FormWhat it is / example
KineticEnergy of motion — wind, flowing water, a moving car (powers turbines & dams).
PotentialStored energy from position/state — water behind a dam, chemical bonds in fuel. Gravitational PE depends on height, mass & gravitational strength.
Thermal (heat)Energy from molecular motion / temperature difference — drives steam turbines.
ChemicalStored in molecular bonds — batteries, food, fossil fuels.
NuclearFrom fission/fusion of atoms — splitting uranium releases heat to make steam.
OthersMechanical (kinetic + potential), electrical, light (photons), magnetic.
◎
Asked as: MCQ "energy due to motion = kinetic", "mechanical energy example = a car moving up a hill", "movement of particles due to temperature = thermal". And Section B: "Explain the Law of Conservation of Energy with an example" (newest paper, 5 marks).

Measuring energy

  • Joule (J) — SI unit. Kilowatt-hour (kWh) — electricity billing (1 kWh = 3.6 million J). BTU — heating/fuel. Calorie — food energy.

Global energy sources (share of consumption)

  • Oil 31% · Coal 27% · Natural gas 24% · Renewables 12% (fastest growing) · Nuclear 6% (zero-carbon but waste challenges).
  • Fossil fuels (oil, gas, coal) = non-renewable, formed over millions of years from plant/animal remains. Reliable, not weather-dependent, but finite & polluting.

Renewable & emerging sources

  • Solar (PV cells / thermal), Wind (turbines), Hydropower (largest renewable electricity source).
  • Emerging: Hydrogen (green H₂ via electrolysis), Geothermal, Bioenergy, Ocean/tidal.
  • Disadvantage of most renewables = unreliable / intermittent supply → needs storage & smart grids.

Energy storage & grid integration

Critical for managing renewable intermittency: battery storage (lithium-ion, flow), pumped hydro, thermal storage, and smart grid technologies for monitoring & control.

Malaysia's energy landscape — the facts examiners love

  • ~90% energy self-sufficiency; energy sector ≈ 15% of GDP (some slides cite up to 28% incl. supply chain); 80,000+ jobs.
  • PETRONAS — state oil & gas company, both operator & regulator, operates in 30+ countries.
  • ~3.6 billion barrels oil, 42 trillion cubic feet gas. ~45% of electricity from coal (mostly imported).
  • Responsible for ~80% of national greenhouse-gas emissions → central to sustainability plans.
  • Target: 31% renewable capacity by 2025, 40% by 2035.
Key Malaysian policies National Energy Transition Roadmap (NETR, 2023) · Net Energy Metering (NEM) · Large Scale Solar (LSS) · Corporate Green Power Programme (CGPP) · Renewable Energy Act 2011 (Feed-in Tariff) · Malaysia Renewable Energy Roadmap (MyRER) · Low Carbon Nation Aspiration 2040.

The four "grid generations" (digitalisation)

  1. Traditional grid — one-way flow, manual control.
  2. Automated grid — digital monitoring, SCADA, automated switching.
  3. Interactive grid — two-way flow, distributed generation, smart meters, demand response.
  4. Intelligent grid — AI-optimised, self-healing, predictive, seamless renewable integration.
◎
Asked as: "smart grid allows a ___ flow of energy and data" → bidirectional. And the definition of a self-healing grid (self-recovers after natural disaster/human error) — this MCQ appears in 4 papers.

Computing in energy — the big picture

Computing = hardware + software + networks + data. In industry it extends to Operational Technology (OT), converging with IT via IoT. Fundamental elements of digital tech: Data · Analytics · Connectivity. Digitalisation functions: Monitor → Analyse → Predict → Operate. Top digital technologies transforming energy: AI/ML, blockchain, IoT, cloud, 5G.

Porter's Value Chain (1985)

Value chain — the full range of activities an organisation performs to create value for customers, from raw materials to final delivery. Every step adds value; analysing them reveals where to cut cost, differentiate, and build competitive advantage.
Primary activitiesSupport activities
Inbound logisticsFirm infrastructure (planning, finance, quality)
Operations / productionHuman Resource management
Outbound logisticsTechnology / ICT development
Marketing & salesProcurement (purchasing inputs)
Service
◎
Classic MCQ trap — "Identify the INVALID statements": statement I ("primary activities are support activities") and statement IV ("value chain shouldn't interact with up/downstream") are both false → answer is I and IV. Also: Procurement = the support activity that acquires inputs; ICT reduces IT cost & keeps systems reliable.

Energy value chain — three stages

Upstream = exploration & extraction · Midstream = processing & transport · Downstream = distribution, retail & consumption.

Electricity value chain (Malaysia)

StageWhat happens
GenerationThermal (coal + gas) ≈78%, hydro ≈17%, solar/renewables ≈5%. Key players: TNB GenCo, IPPs.
TransmissionBulk transfer over the National Grid at 132kV–500kV; run by TNB's Grid System Operator. (Overhead lines cheapest; underground cable where space is tight / submarine.)
DistributionThe "middle mile" — steps voltage down (11–33kV then 240–415V) via 500+ substations & transformers to consumers.
Retail & end-useTariffs: Domestic (TD), Commercial (TC), Industrial (TI), Agriculture (TF), Street lighting (TL). Smart-meter rollout (9M+ meters), myTNB app.

Key players: TNB (Peninsular), Sarawak Energy (SEB), Sabah Electricity (SESB), and IPPs (Independent Power Producers = private companies that generate electricity for sale).

Oil & Gas value chain (Malaysia)

StageDetail
Upstream (E&P)Explore → drill → produce. High risk, high capital, long lead times, tech-intensive. Fields: Tapis, Kikeh, Gumusut-Kakap. Tech: EOR, 3D/4D seismic, deepwater drilling, PSCs.
MidstreamShipping, pipelines, storage. PETRONAS LNG Complex Bintulu (30 MTPA); Peninsular Gas Utilisation (PGU) pipeline >2,500km.
DownstreamRefining, petrochemicals, retail. Refineries: Melaka, Terengganu, RAPID Pengerang; 1,000+ PETRONAS stations.

Renewable energy — five types (know the sub-types)

SourceSub-types / notes
BioenergyBiomass (wood, crop waste) · Biogas (methane from rotting waste/POME/landfill) · Biofuel (bioethanol, biodiesel).
WindUtility-scale · Offshore (stronger, steadier wind) · Distributed (small/residential).
HydropowerRun-of-river · Storage (dam + reservoir) · Pumped storage (peak load) · Offshore (tidal/wave).
SolarPassive (no devices, building design) vs Active (uses air/liquid + panels to collect/store/convert).
GeothermalHeat from beneath the Earth boils water → steam → turbine. Low emissions if water re-injected.

RE integration policies & the future

  • Feed-in Tariff (FiT) — sell RE to utility at a guaranteed premium (Renewable Energy Act 2011).
  • Net Energy Metering (NEM) — install solar for self-use, export excess to the grid (NEM 1.0→3.0).
  • Large Scale Solar (LSS) — competitive bidding; prices fell from RM0.41 → below RM0.24/kWh.
  • Future: smart grids, energy storage, RE expansion (31%/40%), ASEAN Power Grid interconnection.
  • O&G future: digital oilfields, renewables diversification, hydrogen, Carbon Capture & Storage (Kasawari CCS), Net Zero 2050.
◎
Asked as: "RE incentive that lets individuals sell electricity back to the grid" → NEM. "Solar PV panel function" → convert sunlight to electricity. "Active solar system" → uses air/liquid to collect & store. "Temenggor Dam" → hydro power station.

Roles of Computing in the Energy Industry
Blockchain · smart grid & IoT · big data & AI · + the nuclear supplement
+

This is the highest-yield chapter for Section B. Three parts + a nuclear add-on.

Part 1 — Blockchain & security

Blockchain — a distributed ledger that records transactions across many computers. Transparent, immutable, uses consensus mechanisms (no central authority) and cryptographic encryption. Enables direct peer-to-peer energy transactions without intermediaries.

How it works: transaction initiation → block creation → distributed verification (encrypted, copied to many nodes) → consensus confirmation.

  • Components of a blockchain-based energy system: smartphone apps, sensor technology, smart meters (traders & banks are not core) → the recurring MCQ answer is I, III, IV.
  • Electricity uses: peer-to-peer trading (prosumers), billing/micropayments, Renewable Energy Certificates, grid management, EV charging, customer switching.
  • Oil & gas uses: supply-chain tracking, smart contracts (joint ventures, royalties), trading & settlement, compliance/certification.
  • Security benefits: no single point of failure, identity management, data integrity (immutable), transparency/auditability.
  • Challenges: scalability, energy use of consensus, legacy integration, interoperability, unclear regulation.
▣
Case studies to name: Brooklyn Microgrid (Ethereum-based P2P solar trading between neighbours) and the OOC Oil & Gas Blockchain Consortium (Chevron, ExxonMobil, Shell — cut admin costs 30–50%).

Part 2 — Smart grid & IoT

Smart grid — an electricity network that intelligently integrates all users (generators, consumers, prosumers) to deliver sustainable, economic, secure supply. Automated, two-way flow of energy and data, with digital meters, sensors, control systems and analytics.
Internet of Things (IoT) — a network connecting uniquely identifiable "things" (sensors, meters, valves, transformers) to the internet so their state can be monitored and changed anytime, anywhere.

IoT architecture: Edge devices (sensors) → IoT hub (auth, encryption, comms) → Storage → Analytics engines → Actions & presentation (dashboards, alerts, control).

Smart meter — an advanced meter that records consumption in short intervals (15–60 min), communicates automatically to the utility, and supports two-way communication + remote disconnect. TNB plans 9.1 million smart meters. Benefits: accurate billing, outage detection, theft prevention, demand response, renewable integration.
  • IoT in electricity: asset performance management, renewable integration, customer efficiency, demand response, automatic meter reading, outage management.
  • IoT in oil & gas: upstream well/reservoir monitoring, midstream pipeline leak detection, refinery process control, logistics tracking.
  • Smart grid challenges: cybersecurity, legacy integration, regulation lag, privacy of consumption data, high upfront cost/ROI.
◎
Asked as: "compare the roles of smart meters, IoT edge devices & analytics engines" and "how do smart grids improve energy efficiency & reliability?" — plus MCQs on demand response (adjusts demand to match supply) and AI predictive maintenance (detects faults before failure).

Part 3 — Big data & AI

Big Data — the 5 V's Volume (huge amounts) · Velocity (real-time streaming) · Variety (structured + unstructured) · Veracity (quality/accuracy) · Value (turning data into insight). MCQ recurring answer = Variety, Volume, Velocity.
Analytics typeQuestion it answers
DescriptiveWhat happened? (dashboards, reports)
DiagnosticWhy did it happen? (root cause)
PredictiveWhat might happen? (ML forecasting, failure prediction)
PrescriptiveWhat should we do? (optimisation, AI recommendations)
  • Machine learning types: supervised (labelled → forecasting), unsupervised (patterns → anomaly detection), reinforcement (trial & error → trading, control), deep learning (neural nets → seismic, imagery).
  • AI in electricity: load forecasting, grid stability, renewable forecasting, predictive maintenance.
  • AI in oil & gas: exploration (seismic), production optimisation, refinery control, trading & risk.
▣
AI case studies: BP Plant Operations Advisor (200,000+ sensors, −30% unplanned downtime) & Google DeepMind × UK National Grid (+20% wind-forecast accuracy).
Digital Twin — a virtual, physics- and data-based replica of a physical asset/system that models its properties & interactions, enabling simulation, predictive maintenance and optimisation without touching the real system. Example: a digital twin of a wind turbine predicts gearbox failure from live sensor data.

Chapter 3 supplement — the Nuclear industry & computing

Nuclear = controlled fission of uranium → heat → steam → turbine → electricity. Reliable low-carbon baseload power (also medical, research, defence uses).

Carbon Footprint
Carbon / computing / digital footprints · impacts · green computing
+

Carbon footprint

Carbon footprint — the total amount of greenhouse gases (expressed as CO₂) produced directly and indirectly to support human activities. (A greenhouse gas absorbs & emits thermal infrared radiation — CO₂, methane, water vapour, nitrous oxide, ozone. Without them Earth would average about −18°C.)

Main sources for consumers: household energy use, transportation, food & agriculture, and industry (the goods we buy). A footprint maps where energy is used and lost, and the GHGs emitted — revealing efficiency opportunities.

Impacts of carbon footprint

  • Global warming — rising temperatures.
  • Sea-level rise — from melting polar ice caps.
  • Extreme weather events — heavy floods, storms. (Also: loss of biodiversity, ocean acidification.)

Ways to reduce carbon footprint

  • Energy efficiency — efficient appliances, LED bulbs, Energy Star products.
  • Renewable energy — solar, wind, hydro instead of fossil fuels.
  • Sustainable transport — carpooling, public transport, EVs.
  • Waste reduction — recycling, repurposing.

The footprint family — don't confuse these

TermMeaning
Computing footprintEnvironmental impact of producing, using & disposing of computers/IT — energy use, CO₂, e-waste. (Software also has a "memory footprint".)
Digital footprintThe trail of data you leave online (social media, searches, purchases).
Energy footprintThe GHG/climate impact tied to energy consumed.

Where the computing footprint comes from: device energy consumption (data centres, personal devices), manufacturing impact, e-waste disposal, and network/internet infrastructure. ICT ≈ 2% of world CO₂ emissions; energy can be ~30% of data-centre cost.

Green Computing

Green Computing — the environmentally responsible and eco-friendly use of computers & IT resources: designing, engineering, manufacturing, using and disposing of computing devices to reduce environmental impact.

Goals: reduce energy consumption · minimise e-waste · sustainable manufacturing · optimise resource use.

Practices to adopt (great for a "FOUR ways" question):

  • Enable power-saving / sleep modes and buy energy-efficient (high Energy-Star-rated) hardware.
  • Server virtualisation & cloud consolidation to cut data-centre energy.
  • Responsible e-waste recycling at certified centres; repair/reuse/repurpose devices.
  • Reduce printing, use efficient software, and power down idle equipment.
◎
Asked as (newest paper): "Describe the term Green Computing (2m)" + "Describe FOUR ways to adopt green-computing practices to reduce carbon footprint (8m)." Also the e-waste case study (Chapter opener "Computer & Environmental Hazard").

Energy Star & e-waste

  • Energy Star — helps consumers/business save money, protects the environment via energy-efficient products, and identifies top-performing cost-effective products (MCQ answer: all of the above). In Malaysia the rating runs 2★ (minimal) to 5★ (max efficiency).
  • E-waste — old computers contain 1,000+ materials, many toxic (lead, brominated flame retardants/PBDEs). Improper disposal harms health & environment; recovery of gold/copper is valuable.

Model answer frameworks

The recurring long-form questions, with point-by-point skeletons you can adapt. Marks come from structure + named examples, so each frame gives you exactly the points to hit.

"Explain the technology in the related industry and give TWO (2) examples of its application."
Appears in 5/8 papers~10 marks · Ch 3
Structure for each sub-part (define → explain → 2 examples)

(a) Augmented Reality in the nuclear industry. AR overlays digital information onto a technician's real-world view. Examples: (1) guiding maintenance/repair by overlaying step-by-step instructions on reactor equipment; (2) hands-free radiation-zone inspection where dose data appears in the technician's headset. Also usable: operator training simulations

(b) AI / Machine Learning in the digital oilfield. AI analyses sensor & seismic data to predict and optimise. Examples: (1) predictive maintenance of pumps/wells to prevent downtime; (2) reservoir/production optimisation and seismic interpretation to locate reserves.

(c) Blockchain in the electricity industry. A distributed ledger enabling trusted peer-to-peer transactions. Examples: (1) P2P solar trading between neighbours (Brooklyn Microgrid); (2) automated billing/micropayments via smart contracts + smart meters, or Renewable Energy Certificate tracking.

✎
Whatever pairing they give (mobile computing in oilfield, drones in O&G, ML in nuclear…), use the same frame: 1 sentence defining the tech → 1 sentence on its role in that industry → 2 concrete examples. That's the full 5 marks.
Nuclear power: challenges, TNB going nuclear (advantages/disadvantages), and public acceptance.
Appears in 3/8 papersCh 3 nuclear
Two challenges for the nuclear sector
  • Safety & accident risk — radiation leaks (Chernobyl/Fukushima memory) demand heavy regulation and skilled staff.
  • Radioactive waste management — long-lived waste needs secure long-term disposal facilities.
If TNB used nuclear — two advantages / two disadvantages

Advantages: reliable low-carbon baseload power; diversifies supply & reduces fossil-fuel dependence. Disadvantages: very high build cost & long lead time; waste storage + public-safety concerns.

Biggest challenge for TNB — justify

Public acceptance / safety perception (or the huge upfront cost). Justify: without public trust and regulatory approval, no site can be built regardless of the engineering case.

Measures to raise public acceptance
  • Transparent public education on safety & modern reactor design.
  • Independent regulation, community engagement, and clear emergency/waste plans.
Upstream / Midstream / Downstream — functions + how to digitise (Petronas system-analyst).
Appears in 3/8 papersCh 2
Functions
  • Upstream (E&P): explore for and extract crude oil & gas — seismic surveys, drilling, production. High risk/high capital.
  • Midstream: move & store the raw hydrocarbons — pipelines, shipping/LNG tankers, storage terminals, trading.
  • Downstream: refine into products & sell — refining, petrochemicals, distribution, retail stations.
Two ways to digitise (pick one segment)

Upstream example: (1) IoT sensors + AI for real-time well/reservoir monitoring and predictive maintenance; (2) digital twin of the drilling platform for simulation & optimisation. Midstream: pipeline leak-detection sensors + GIS. Downstream: AI process control in refineries; app-based retail/payment.

Case study (Section C): "How do digitalisation + deregulation create a smarter, more competitive Malaysian energy market?"
Newest paper · 30 marksCh 1–4 applied
Define the two terms

Deregulation = opening the monopolistic electricity market to competition so multiple retailers can offer better prices/services. Digitalisation = integrating digital technologies (IoT, AI, blockchain, big data) throughout the value chain to transform operations (not just converting analogue to digital = "digitisation").

How digitalisation helps TNB (give 2 examples)
  • Smart meters → real-time usage insight, demand monitoring, better resource allocation.
  • AI predictive maintenance on the grid/solar farms → fewer outages, lower repair cost.
The 10-mark part — how they reinforce each other (need 3+ points)
  • Digital platforms/apps let consumers compare prices & switch providers → makes a deregulated market work.
  • New entrants (RE start-ups) use blockchain P2P trading & AI storage optimisation to compete → deregulation accelerates digital adoption.
  • Together: efficiency + transparency + consumer empowerment (digitalisation) meets competition + innovation + sustainability (deregulation).

Note the balance too: deregulation risks unstable prices & small firms being squeezed; digitalisation raises cybersecurity risk. Mentioning a trade-off shows analysis and earns top-band marks. Carbon footprint / Green computing / IoT — the shorter Section B staples.
Appears across 4+ papersCh 3 & 4
Carbon footprint — impacts + reduction

Impacts: global warming, sea-level rise, extreme weather. Reduce via energy efficiency, renewables, sustainable transport, waste reduction. (For a sector like healthcare: switch to renewable-powered facilities + digitise records to cut paper/energy.)

Green Computing — define + FOUR practices

Define: eco-friendly design, use & disposal of IT. Four practices: sleep/power modes + efficient hardware · server virtualisation/cloud · certified e-waste recycling & reuse · reduce printing / power down idle devices.

IoT — data types collected + energy examples

IoT devices collect operational data — temperature, vibration, pressure, humidity, electrical parameters, consumption. Used for predictive maintenance, demand response, real-time control. Energy examples: (1) smart meters reporting household consumption; (2) pipeline/wellhead sensors monitoring flow & leaks.
